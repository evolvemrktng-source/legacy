Commercial Construction in Metro Detroit:
What Business Owners Need to Know Before They Build

Commercial Construction · 7 min read

Building a commercial space in Metro Detroit is one of the most significant investments a business owner will make. Whether you’re opening a restaurant, building a gas station, expanding a medical clinic, or developing a retail plaza, the decisions you make before the first shovel goes in the ground will determine whether the project finishes on time, on budget, and to the standard your business requires. Here’s what you need to understand going in.

Permitting Is Not a Formality

The single most common source of delays on commercial projects in Metro Detroit is the permitting process — specifically, submitting drawings that aren’t permit-ready. Each municipality has its own requirements, and the City of Detroit, Wayne County, Oakland County, and Macomb County jurisdictions each have their own review timelines and submission standards.

Drawings that come back for revision add weeks or months to a project before a single wall is framed. Working with a design team that knows local municipal requirements — and that has relationships with plan reviewers — dramatically reduces this risk. It’s one of the most undervalued advantages of working with a locally based design-build firm.

Understand What MEP Actually Covers

MEP stands for mechanical, electrical, and plumbing — the three building systems that make a structure functional. For commercial projects, MEP is where the complexity lives. A restaurant requires grease trap sizing, hood exhaust calculations, and fire suppression coordination. A medical clinic requires specialized electrical loads, medical gas infrastructure, and specific plumbing fixture counts. A gas station involves underground fuel system engineering and environmental compliance on top of standard MEP.

Many general contractors outsource MEP design to subcontractors who weren’t involved in the architectural process. When the MEP drawings arrive late — or don’t coordinate with the architectural plans — the project stalls. A design-build firm that handles MEP in-house eliminates this gap by integrating the systems into the design from the start.

Budget for the Full Scope, Not Just Construction

One of the most common mistakes commercial project owners make is budgeting only for construction costs and treating architecture, engineering, and permitting fees as afterthoughts. These are not afterthoughts. They are the foundation on which a permit is issued and a building is built.

A realistic commercial construction budget includes design and engineering fees, permit fees, utility connection costs, site preparation, construction, and a contingency reserve — typically 10 to 15 percent of the total project cost — for conditions discovered during construction that weren’t visible during the design phase. Any contractor who quotes you a number without accounting for all of these categories is giving you an incomplete picture.

Timeline Expectations for Commercial Projects in Metro Detroit

Ground-up commercial construction in Metro Detroit typically runs 6 to 18 months depending on project complexity, size, and permit jurisdiction. A straightforward restaurant build-out in an existing shell space can move in 3 to 5 months. A ground-up apartment building or factory takes considerably longer.

The design and permitting phase alone can take 2 to 4 months before construction begins. Owners who plan for this upfront — and who don’t compress the design phase to save time — typically finish faster overall, because they spend less time resolving field coordination issues that should have been caught on paper.

Choosing the Right Contractor

The right commercial contractor for a Metro Detroit project is licensed, insured, experienced in your specific project type, and capable of communicating clearly throughout the build. Ask for project references in the same category as your build. Ask who will be on site daily. Ask how change orders are handled and documented.

If your contractor can’t answer those questions clearly and specifically, that’s a signal. The right contractor can — because they’ve built the same type of project before and they manage it with a defined process, not instinct.
