MEP Systems Explained:
Why Mechanical, Electrical, and Plumbing Design Makes or Breaks a Construction Project

MEP · 7 min read

When most people think about a construction project, they think about what they can see — the facade, the layout, the finishes. What actually makes a building function is invisible. It’s inside the walls, above the ceiling, and below the floor: the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems that control airflow, power, and water throughout a structure. Getting MEP right is not a detail. It is the difference between a building that works and one that doesn’t.

What MEP Stands For

MEP is shorthand for mechanical, electrical, and plumbing — the three primary building system disciplines that every commercial and industrial construction project requires. Each one is a field of engineering in its own right, and each one has to be designed in coordination with the other two and with the architectural layout of the building.

Mechanical covers HVAC — heating, ventilation, and air conditioning — including ductwork layout, rooftop equipment, controls, and airflow calculations. Electrical covers power distribution, lighting, panel schedules, load calculations, and specialty systems like fire alarm and data infrastructure. Plumbing covers supply lines, waste and vent systems, fixture counts, and in some project types, medical gas or process piping.

Why MEP Coordination Is Where Projects Fail

The most common source of construction delays and cost overruns on commercial and industrial projects is MEP coordination failure. This happens when the mechanical engineer, electrical engineer, and plumber are each working from the architectural drawings but not working with each other — or not working with the contractor who will actually install the systems.

The result is clashes. A duct run that conflicts with a structural beam. An electrical panel located where a plumbing chase needs to be. A rooftop HVAC unit that the structural system wasn’t designed to support. Each of these conflicts has to be resolved in the field, which means work stops, decisions are made under pressure, and someone pays for the rework.

When MEP is coordinated in-house — by a team that is also doing the architectural and structural design — these conflicts are caught on paper, not on the job site.

MEP for Restaurants and Food Service Facilities

Food service is one of the most MEP-intensive commercial build types. A restaurant kitchen requires a Type I hood exhaust system with makeup air, a grease interceptor sized for the kitchen volume, a fire suppression system, a gas line with the correct BTU load, and a grease drain system that meets local health department requirements — all before the dining room finishes are selected.

Permitting a restaurant in Metro Detroit requires all of these systems to be documented in the construction drawings and reviewed by the building department, the fire marshal, and in some jurisdictions the health department. Drawings that don’t capture all of it come back for revision. Revisions take time. Time is money, especially when you’re paying rent on a space that isn’t open yet.

MEP for Industrial and Factory Builds

Industrial MEP is a different challenge entirely. Factory builds require electrical systems designed around machinery loads that may not be fully specified until late in the design process. HVAC has to account for process heat loads, ventilation requirements for specific operations, and in some facilities, explosion-proof equipment requirements.

Industrial plumbing often includes process piping — compressed air lines, coolant systems, floor drains designed for specific waste types — in addition to standard domestic plumbing. All of this has to be engineered, coordinated, and permitted before construction begins.

What In-House MEP Means for Your Project

When your design-build contractor handles MEP in-house, the engineering team is in the same building as the architects. Coordination meetings happen in real time. When a duct run conflicts with a structural member, it’s caught before the drawings are issued for permit. When the electrical panel location needs to move, the architectural drawings are updated the same day.

This is not the standard experience on projects where MEP is outsourced to external subcontractors. In that model, coordination happens by email between firms that have other clients and other priorities. Things fall through the cracks. The project owner pays for it.
